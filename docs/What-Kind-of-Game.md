# What Kind of Game Is This?

Your concept will land better if you arrive knowing the register.

---

## The short version

This is **industrial-occult noir**. Quiet dread, moral weight, texture. The work is interesting because it costs something. You are not heroes in the traditional sense — you are professionals doing a hard job in a world that does not acknowledge the job exists.

---

## What it sounds like in play

Here is the kind of moment this campaign is built to produce:

*The cold storage locker in the basement holds seventeen items. You have not opened drawer fourteen in four months. You know what's in it. You know what it's waiting for. You open it anyway because the new job requires it — and when you do, you realize the item has been slowly, incrementally moving in a way you cannot account for, because the cold is supposed to stop that, and the cold has always stopped that.*

Here is another:

*You pull the **Anchor Item** from the target — the keepsake that lets a hidden creature pass as human — a silver locket worn smooth by someone's thumb, the photograph inside long since gone to a blur of faces. The target is neutralized. You file the paperwork. In the ledger, you write the locket's description. You write the date. You do not write what the date on the inside lid means about how long this has been going on.*

And another:

*Consequence the cat is asleep on the contract. She has been asleep on every contract you have received in two years. She has never helped with anything. She is always there.*

---

## What it does not sound like

- *"That was awesome."* (Not the wrong word — just the wrong register. We reach for *interesting* or *costly* before we reach for *cool.*)
- Quips that undercut a moment that deserves silence. (Everywhere else, jokes are welcome — see below.)
- Monsters that are only threats. Every creature in this world has a history that the crew may never fully learn.
- NPCs whose grief is loud. Tragedy in this campaign is buried. The wedding ring is mentioned once and not mentioned again; the player decides whether to pursue it.

---

## And yes — this game is funny

Think *monster-of-the-week*: real dread, real heart, and then something absurd that you did not see coming. Expect banter, bad plans that go wrong in loud ways, a cat who is always asleep on the evidence, and an unreasonable amount of paperwork. The funny parts are *obviously* funny. The quiet parts are quiet. Laugh when it's funny; go still when it isn't. **Come in ready to make the table laugh** — your character's worst idea is a gift.

---

## Your character is not special in the cinematic sense

You are good at the work. You have survived things that should not be survivable. But you do not have a destiny. The campaign is not building toward your apotheosis.

What you have instead:

- A **Vice** that you return to under pressure. You will play this, and it will not be pretty.
- A **Cursed Heirloom** that the GM has quietly wired to something you haven't found yet.
- A **Friend and a Rival** who will appear in play within the first few sessions, with their own agendas.
- A **history with this firm** — you have been doing this work for two years. You have stories you don't tell.

When you make your character, ask: *what has two years of this work done to them?* Not "how cool are they" but "what are they carrying?" That question will generate better play than any ability selection.

---

## The cover-up

The world does not know monsters are real. The Ministry enforces this actively. The crew's cover — a failing bookstore — exists to maintain it.

This means: **a witness with a phone is a more serious complication than a wound.** A Ministry internal audit is more dangerous than a creature. Exposure does not end a session; it can end careers, licenses, and in some circumstances, more than that.

Take the cover-up seriously and your character will have the right relationship to the world.

---

## What you are selling

You keep what you find and sell it on a black market the Ministry doesn't know exists. This is not morally neutral — some of what you sell has a name and a history, and the person who buys it has reasons you may not want to know.

The campaign does not ask you to resolve whether this is wrong. It asks you to do it and notice what you feel.

---

## On pacing and atmosphere

Some scenes will be slow. Deliberately slow. The kind of slow where you are not sure the scene is *doing* anything — until two sessions later, when you realize it was doing everything.

When a scene feels quiet: stay in it. Ask what your character notices. Describe what they do with their hands. The campaign rewards players who are willing to inhabit the texture of a moment rather than push through it to the next roll.

---

*Build a character who fits a world that is grey, procedural, and quietly unsettling. Leave room in your concept for them to be changed by the work. That's the game.*
